The Icehouse

The Firebreak was one night of too much heat. This room is a whole winter spent hoarding cold. Below a country house there is a pond, and in the shrubbery a brick ice house sunk in the ground like an egg. Every day from the first of December you can send the gang out onto the ice to cut and cart, or wait for a harder frost and thicker ice. Thin ice and ice cut in a thaw pack loosely and melt all summer. Then the year runs on to Michaelmas, the kitchen draws on what you saved, and the page shows what the same year would have cost if you had known the weather in advance.

Day
—

Every day is a different estate, year and winter: some hard, some ordinary, and some so mild the pond barely skins over.

What is real here and what is not

Ice houses, ice cut from estate ponds, straw packing, the importance of cutting thick, dry ice, and Norway ice shipped to British ports from the 1820s are all real. The estates, the winters, the gangs, the prices and the household's wants are the day's number.

  1. 01

    A cellar for the cold

    From the late seventeenth century, and above all in the eighteenth and nineteenth, a country house of any size had an ice house: a brick chamber, often shaped like an egg standing on its point, sunk into a bank in the shade with a drain at the bottom and a passage with two or three doors to keep the warm air out. Filled well, it could keep ice from one winter to the next.

  2. 02

    Getting the ice in

    The ice came from the nearest pond, lake or flooded field. When the frost had held long enough, the garden staff and estate labourers broke it into slabs, carted it up, and rammed it down in layers, sometimes with straw between, as hard and tight as they could. Gardeners' manuals said to take thick ice in dry, hard weather: wet or broken ice left air spaces, and the store melted from the inside.

  3. 03

    Norway ice

    In mild winters English ponds might hardly freeze at all. From 1822 ships brought ice cut on Norwegian lakes into London, and later into many other ports, and an estate could buy clean block ice from a merchant. By the end of the century imported ice and then ice machines had made the old ice houses unnecessary.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    The pond is a grid of squares that thicken with the frost by a standard rule (ice grows as the square root of the frost added up day by day) and thin in a thaw. The house melts more slowly when it is fuller and when the ice is good, and faster in hot weather. The wages, prices and the household's wants are sensible guesses, not the accounts of any real house.

Sixty-nine rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it chooses a winter, freezes a pond a night at a time and decides who is coming to dinner in August; what you put away in January is what the kitchen has to work with.
