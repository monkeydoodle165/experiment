The Threshing Barn

The Cider Mill finished at Martinmas, when the men were wanted for the threshing. This room is that threshing: the long winter between Martinmas and Lady Day on a corn farm, when the harvest stands in ricks in the yard and the men beat it out on the barn floor with flails, a few quarters a man a week. There is one floor, so one rick at a time. The rats live in the ricks and eat all winter. The half-year’s rent is due at Christmas whatever the price of corn is doing, and the corn market rises, falls or wanders as it likes. Sometimes the travelling threshing machine comes round, and does a fortnight’s work in a week, but the men it puts out of work go on the parish, and the parish is you.

Selling does not use up the week: cart as much of the threshed corn to market as you like, at this week’s price, unless snow or floods have shut the roads. Choosing a rick does: the men thresh it all week at so much a quarter, slower in the damp and in the short week at Christmas, and the threshed corn goes into the granary to wait for a sale. The rats take something from every rick every week, more in hard weather and much less from a rick built on staddle stones; in the granary they take very little. The merchant’s word on the trade is about next week, and mostly right. The rent comes out at Christmas; if you are short, the bank lends it and charges for it until it is paid back. When the machine is in the parish, press Hire and then choose the rick it threshes. Keys: 1–5 thresh that rick, M the machine, 0 let the week go by. Point at the strip to see any week.

Day
—

Every day is a different parish and winter: a rising market that rewards holding the corn back, a falling one that punishes it, a hard winter that shuts the roads and drives the rats into the ricks, and the odd year, 1830, when no machine dares come.

What is real here and what is not

Corn ricks, staddle stones, flail threshing on the barn floor through the winter, threshers paid by the quarter, travelling threshing machines, the Christmas rent day and the Swing riots of 1830 are all real, and so are the parishes: each is a corn-growing place with a great barn. The farms, the ricks, the men, the prices and the weather are the day’s number.

  1. 01

    The flail

    Two sticks joined by a leather hinge: the handstaff and the swingel. Men worked in pairs or fours on the barn floor between the great doors, which were opened to let the wind carry the chaff off when the corn was winnowed. It was the main winter work of the farm labourer, and a slow one: a good man might thresh a quarter of wheat or rather more of barley or oats in a long day.

  2. 02

    The rick

    Unthreshed corn kept in the sheaf, built into ricks and thatched. Rats and mice lived in them and could eat a large share of a rick over a winter; the cure was to build the rick on a frame raised on staddle stones, the mushroom-shaped stones the rats could not climb. Farmers threshed through the winter partly to have work for their men and partly to catch the market.

  3. 03

    The machine

    Horse-driven threshing machines spread through the south and east from the 1790s, often hired from a man who took them from farm to farm. They threshed far faster and cheaper, and they took away the labourers’ winter work. In the autumn and winter of 1830 the Swing riots broke hundreds of them across southern England. Steam threshing tackle followed from the 1850s.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    The men thresh at fixed rates, the rats take a fixed share, every sale clears a whole crop at one price, and the market moves as one trade with each grain wandering a little off it. There is no winnowing, no straw, no seed corn kept back, no tithe and no Corn Laws, and every rate, rent and price is a sensible guess, not the books of any real farm.

Seventy-six rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it books the machine for the week before Christmas, sends the snow on market day, and keeps the best prices of the winter for the week after you sold.
