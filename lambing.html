The Lambing Fold

The Decoy spent the winter waiting on the wild fowl. This room is the end of that winter, when the downland flocks lambed: a fold of wattle hurdles on the open down, a row of little pens thatched with straw along its sheltered side, and the shepherd's hut on its iron wheels beside it, where he lived for six weeks. Ewes lamb at any hour, and the ones that matter are the ones that lamb at two in the morning in a frost. You are the shepherd. Each evening you look at the sky and the ewes and choose how many times to get up in the night. Every round with the lantern may save a lamb; every round also costs you sleep you will need next week.

Rounds are spread evenly between ten at night and six in the morning, and dawn finds everything the rounds missed. A lamb found soon is rubbed down and penned with its mother; one left in the cold may be dead by the next round, and a lamb laid wrong needs your hand within the hour. A full night's sleep sets you up again; more than one round a night wears you down, and a worn-out shepherd walks past what he is looking for. The quiet-nights button sleeps until a ewe looks close or the weather turns, a week at most. Keys: 0–4 choose the night's rounds, Q sleeps through the quiet nights. Point at the chart to see any night.

Day
—

Every day is a different farm, flock and spring: some lambings that come all in a rush with too few pens, some long and easy, and some hard springs when the snow comes in the third week, just as the fold is busiest.

What is real here and what is not

Lambing folds, hurdles, thatched pens, shepherd's huts, and the breeds are all real, and so are the places: each is a sheep country where flocks like these lambed in the nineteenth century. The flocks, the shepherds, the weather and every lambing are the day's number.

  1. 01

    The fold on the down

    On the chalk downs of Sussex, Hampshire, Wiltshire and Dorset, and on the Cotswolds and the wolds, the flock was brought into a lambing fold in the new year: a square of wattle hurdles pitched in a sheltered spot, often with straw or furze packed into them against the wind, and a row of small pens along one side, roofed with hurdles and thatch, where a ewe and her new lambs could be shut in for a few days to get to know each other.

  2. 02

    The hut and the lantern

    The shepherd lived beside the fold in a hut on iron wheels, with a stove, a bunk, his medicines and his dog, for the whole of the lambing. He went round the ewes with a lantern through the night. A ewe in difficulty, with a lamb laid wrong, needed help quickly; a lamb born into a frost or a wet night had to be found, rubbed dry and got sucking, or it chilled and died before morning.

  3. 03

    Sleep

    Shepherds kept going through the lambing on snatched sleep, and every account of it talks about tiredness. A good shepherd judged which ewes were near their time, by their udders, their restlessness and their going off alone, and slept when he could. The measure of his season was the number of lambs reared for every hundred ewes put to the ram.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    Each night is one weather, and rounds are spread evenly through it. Daylight lambings are always attended. One number stands for the shepherd's tiredness: rounds raise it and a full night's sleep brings it down, and the more tired he is the more likely he is to miss a ewe on a round. Pens hold a family for three nights. The rates are sensible guesses, not the records of any real flock.

Seventy-two rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it chooses a farm and a spring, puts the ewes to lamb at two in the morning in a frost, and asks how much sleep you can spare.
