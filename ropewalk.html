The Ropewalk

In the Bakehouse everything had to be fitted into one long fall of heat. Here the work is a single walk, a fifth of a mile long and taken backwards. A boy turns a wheel at one end; you wrap a bundle of hemp round your waist, hook a few fibres to the wheel and walk away from it, letting the hemp out through your fingers. The wheel puts in the twist. How hard the yarn comes out depends on how fast you walk, and how thick it comes out depends on how fast you let the hemp go. You have one body to keep both even with, all the way to the far end.

    Every day is a different walk, hemp, boy on the wheel and rope on the slate.

    What is real here and what is not

    The way hemp was spun on a walk, and why a spinner had to match his pace to the wheel, are real. The walk, the hemp, the boy and the order are the day's number; the yarn is a small model, sized to make the right mistakes.

    1. 01

      Spinning on a walk

      Before machines took it over in the nineteenth century, rope yarn was spun by hand on a ropewalk: a long straight shed or open alley, often a quarter of a mile, with a spinning wheel at one end. A boy turned the wheel, which drove a set of whirls with hooks on them. The spinner carried a bundle of dressed hemp round his waist, hooked a few fibres on, and walked backwards down the walk, drawing the fibres out of the bundle with one hand while the other, often holding a scrap of wet cloth, smoothed the yarn and took the twist as it ran up to him. The finished yarn was hung on stakeheads, posts with pegs set at intervals all the way down, to keep it off the ground.

    2. 02

      Two things to keep even

      The wheel turns at whatever pace the boy gives it, and every turn goes into the length of yarn just spun. So the twist in each foot is the turns of the wheel divided by the feet walked, and a spinner who kept the same pace while the wheel slowed made soft yarn, while one who stood still made a hard, kinked place. The thickness is the hemp let out divided by the feet walked, so walking faster to keep the twist right meant letting the hemp go faster too. Too little twist and the fibres slide past each other and the yarn parts; too much and the fibres are cut by their own angle and the yarn is weaker for it.

    3. 03

      From yarn to rope

      Yarns were twisted together the other way into strands, and strands the first way again into rope, with a grooved wooden cone called a top travelling down the walk between them to close them up evenly. The opposing twists are what hold a rope together. Laying takes up length, so a rope comes out a quarter to a third shorter than its yarns, more for a hard lay than a soft one. The naval dockyards tested their rope by loading lengths of it to destruction, and a rope breaks at its weakest place however good the rest is.

    4. 04

      What the page simplifies

      Here three yarns make the whole rope, where a real one has dozens or hundreds of yarns in each strand, and they go straight into the rope without being made into strands first. The spinner walks several times faster than anyone could. The twist goes only into the foot under your hand, where in a real yarn it runs back along the length still spun, and the strength of each foot is a simple sum of how much hemp is in it and how near its twist is to the sample's. The kinds of hemp are real; the numbers given to them, and the pay, are the day's number.

    Fifty-nine rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it lays out a walk, hackles the hemp well or badly, puts a boy on the wheel and decides when he will look out of the door.
