The Dairy

The Hayfield was all open sky and guessing the weather. This room is a cold stone dairy on the shady side of a farmhouse, and the weather you are judging is inside a barrel. Cream that is too cold will not turn to butter if you churn it all morning. Cream that is too warm turns quickly, but the butter is soft and greasy and half the fat runs away in the buttermilk. Get it right and somewhere past half an hour the churn goes heavy, then quiet, and then the butter breaks.

What is real here and what is not

The steps, the reason the temperature matters, the way a churn sounds as it comes, and the old way of judging butter out of a hundred are real. The farm, the herd, the cream and the order are the day's number, and the churning is a small model.

  1. 01

    What churning does

    Cream is fat in tiny globules, each one wrapped in a thin skin, floating in watery milk. Knocking it about beats air into it, and the skins break against the bubbles. The cream swells and thickens and the churn goes heavy and dull. Then the bare fat starts to stick together, the foam falls in, and all at once the cream separates into specks of butter in thin buttermilk. Dairymaids called this the butter breaking, or coming, and could hear it: the thud of thick cream turns back into a splash.

  2. 02

    Why the thermometer matters

    Butterfat is a mixture of fats that melt at different temperatures, so at any warmth some of it is solid and some liquid. Too cold and the globules are too hard to stick together, and the churn can go on for hours. Too warm and they stick at once, but the butter is soft, greasy and pale, and fat is lost in the buttermilk. In winter, when cows are on hay, their butterfat is harder and the cream has to be churned several degrees warmer than in summer. Dairy books of the time gave a range, not one figure, and dairymaids learned the rest by ear.

  3. 03

    Washing, salting and working

    Churning stopped when the grains were about the size of wheat, small enough to wash, large enough not to run out with the buttermilk. Buttermilk left in butter goes sour and makes it go off, so the grains were washed in cold water, then salted and pressed on a butter worker to squeeze out water and spread the salt. Work it too little and it weeps and comes out streaky; too much and the grain is broken and it goes greasy. Shows and buyers scored butter out of a hundred: forty-five for flavour, twenty-five for body, fifteen for colour, ten for salting and five for the make-up.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    How fast the cream churns is one number that rises steeply with warmth around a best temperature set by the season and the herd, and with how ripe the cream is and how hard you turn. Too fast and the cream rides round with the barrel instead of falling. The gas that builds up in the first few minutes, the heat your turning adds, the fat lost in the buttermilk, the washing and the working are all rough rules of thumb, and the scorecard is a fair imitation of a judge, not a real one.

Sixty-one rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it chooses a herd and a season, ripens the cream a little more or a little less than it should be, and leaves you to find the right warmth from a book that gives a range and a churn that tells you by sound.
