The Saltpan

The Dairy wanted the cream kept cool and judged by the degree. This room is a long shed full of steam over an iron pan the size of a barn floor, and the same rule runs backwards: here the heat decides the grain. Boil the brine hard and the salt falls as fine as flour, right for the table. Keep it barely warm for days and it grows into big hollow crystals for the herring curers. The order on the slate wants one grain, and the boat will not wait.

What is real here and what is not

The open pan, the way its heat set the grain, the four kinds of salt, the scale and the burned plates are real. The works, the order, the pan and the wind are the day's number, and the pan itself is a small model.

  1. 01

    Brine, a pan and a fire

    Through most of the nineteenth century, almost all British salt was made the same way. Brine was pumped up from salt beds deep underground in Cheshire and Worcestershire and run into wide, shallow iron pans riveted together from plates, set over coal furnaces whose flues ran the whole length underneath. Once the brine is saturated, every ton of water that steams off leaves salt behind on the bottom, and men called wallers raked or lifted it out. Coast works boiled sea water, or sea water strengthened with rock salt, and burned far more coal for every ton.

  2. 02

    Heat makes the grain

    Fast evaporation makes many crystals at once, and they stay small. Slow evaporation makes few, and they grow. So one works made different salts in different pans: fine lump salt boiled hard at about 226°F, the boiling point of saturated brine, and drawn every few hours; common salt at 160 to 180°F; and coarse fishery salt at 100 to 130°F, left for days so the crystals grew into hollow, stepped "hoppers" that dissolved slowly into fish. The wind mattered too: a strong wind over the chimney pulls the fire harder.

  3. 03

    Scale and burned plates

    Brine carries lime as well as salt, and as the water goes the lime settles on the plates as a hard crust of scale (mostly calcium sulphate). Scale keeps the heat from the brine, so the plate under it runs hotter, and a plate under thick scale over a hard fire could go red and split. Pans were let down regularly and men climbed in to break it off with picks, a job called scratching the pan. Hot lump pans needed it far more often than slow fishery pans.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    The grain size is one formula of the temperature, with a fixed spread, and crystals in a cool pan grow a little for each hour they lie there. Evaporation is a steep curve of temperature plus a little help from the wind. The furnace, the draught, the scale, the risk to the plate, the coal and the wages are rules of thumb, chosen to behave sensibly rather than measured. Prices are only rough guides to their period.

Sixty-two rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it picks a works and a brine, writes an order on the slate, and sends the wind across the chimney top hour by hour, so the fire you set is never quite the fire you get.
