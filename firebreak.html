The Firebreak

The Lamplighter carried a small flame round a town to make it safe for the night. This room is the night that goes wrong. It is the small hours in an eighteenth-century market town, a roof is alight, and there is a wind. You have the parish engine, a crew with long iron hooks for pulling houses down, and a second engine on the road from the next town. Water the fire where you can and starve it where you cannot, and when the last roof is out the page shows you what the same fire would have done if nobody had got out of bed.

Pick an engine or the hook crew, then click the town. An engine goes where it is sent and plays on the three-by-three block round that spot, cooling the houses and putting out what is burning, but only as hard as its bucket chain can feed it: the farther from a well or the river, the weaker it plays. The hook crew pulls down the houses you click, in order, so nothing is left there to burn; click one again to take it off the list. Houses glow before they catch. Keys: 1 2 3 pick, Space rings the bell and then holds or releases the clock, Esc clears the hook list.

Every day is a different town, year, street plan, wind and place for the fire to start.

What is real here and what is not

Thatch, timber and brick catching at different heats, fire running downwind and throwing burning thatch over the streets, parish engines fed by bucket chains, and fire hooks for pulling houses down are all real. The towns are real places, but tonight's fire, its street plan and every number in the account are the day's number.

  1. 01

    Towns that burned

    A market town of thatch and timber with a bakehouse, a maltings and a forge in among the houses was always one careless night from disaster. Warwick lost much of its centre in 1694 and Blandford Forum most of the town in 1731; both were rebuilt in brick and stone, which is why they look as they do now.

  2. 02

    The engine

    A parish engine was a wooden cistern on wheels with a hand pump worked by men on long handles, throwing a jet through a leather hose or a swivelling pipe. Its cistern had to be kept full by a line of people passing leather buckets from the nearest well, pump or river, so an engine far from water could do little.

  3. 03

    Hooks and firebreaks

    Churches kept great iron hooks on long poles, and ropes, for tearing the thatch and timbers off houses in the fire's path. A gap of a few houses could stop a fire that no engine could, at the price of the houses, and arguments about who should pay for them went on for years afterwards.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    The town is a grid, each square burns as one, the wind shifts once, and there are only two engines and one crew. Heat, burning times and values are sensible guesses. Pulled-down houses cost their full value and quenched ones count as partly lost, however little the fire touched them.

Sixty-eight rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it lays out a town, puts a candle down in the wrong stable and decides which way the wind blows the thatch. Whether the church is still there in the morning is up to you.
