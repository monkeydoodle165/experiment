The Decoy

The Water Meadow kept the grass growing for the sheep through the winter. This room spends the same winter on the fowl that come in over the marsh. A duck decoy is a quiet pond in a wood with long curving ditches, the pipes, running out from it under netting on hoops. A little dog slips in and out between reed screens along a pipe, and the wild fowl, curious, swim after him up it; then the decoyman shows himself behind them, they rise into the wind, and the pipe narrows to a purse net. You are the decoyman. Each morning you read the wind and choose a pipe, or keep away.

Click a pipe to choose it. Fowl swim up a pipe and rise into the wind, so work the one the wind blows down; with the wind at your back the whole pond smells you. Every drive leaves the fowl that stay a little warier, and only quiet days settle them. Several quiet days at a time stop early when something happens. Keys: 1–6 choose a pipe, W works it, R or Space keeps away for a day.

Day
—

Every day is a different decoy, year and winter: some with a pipe for every wind, some with a gap the wind keeps finding, and some hard winters when the frost fills the pond twice over.

What is real here and what is not

Duck decoys, their pipes, screens and purse nets, the decoy dog, the decoyman's peat and his care for the wind are all real, and so are the places: each is a decoy that was worked in the nineteenth century. The winters, the pipes' directions, the dogs, the numbers of fowl and the prices are the day's number.

  1. 01

    A pond in a wood

    The decoy came to England from Holland in the seventeenth century and spread through the fens, the Essex marshes and the Norfolk and Suffolk coasts. A pond of two or three acres was hidden in trees and reeds, and from it ran four to eight pipes, ditches that curved away and narrowed under netting stretched on iron or willow hoops, ending in a detachable purse net. Some decoys sent thousands of fowl a season to London.

  2. 02

    The dog and the screens

    Along the outside of each pipe stood a row of overlapping reed screens. The decoyman, out of sight behind them, sent his dog, often a small red dog that looked like a fox, in and out between the screens. Ducks follow a fox to mob it, and they swam up the pipe after the dog. Then the man stepped out behind them. Fowl get up into the wind, so they flew up the pipe away from him, into the narrowing net.

  3. 03

    The wind and the quiet

    That is why a decoy had several pipes: one to work in each wind. The man carried smouldering peat to hide his scent, and a pipe with the wind wrong was never touched. Silence mattered more than anything: no shooting, no dogs barking, no work near the pond, and an owner did all he could to keep wildfowlers and their guns well away. Hard frosts brought great flocks over from the Continent, and fetched the best prices.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    One pipe is worked a day, and the wind holds all day. The fowl are one flock of mixed duck, teal and wigeon, with one wariness that rises with every drive, and more with the wrong wind, and falls on quiet days. New fowl come in at a rate that rises into midwinter and jumps in frost; wary fowl leave sooner. The rates and prices are sensible guesses, not the books of any real decoy.

Seventy-one rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it chooses a decoy and a winter, turns the wind round into the east and sends the fowl over the sea; what you leave alone in November is what comes up the pipe in January.
