The Exchange

Today's number has laid out a trading world: eight or nine towns on a road network, eight goods, and a few dozen merchants with a purse each and no instructions. Nobody sets a price here. Every town simply makes some things and eats others, and what it has in its warehouse against what it gets through in a season is the whole of the pricing. The merchants read those numbers, buy where a thing is cheap, haul it somewhere it is dear, and in doing so quietly drag the two prices together.

    Where the prices come from

    1. 01

      The land decides

      Every town is dropped on a terrain — coast, plain, hills, mountain, forest or river crossing — and the terrain decides what it is any good at making. Fish come off coasts, iron out of mountains, wool off pasture, cloth and glass out of the crowded river towns. Each place ends up decent at three things and dependent on the rest.

    2. 02

      The warehouse decides

      Production runs in every day, consumption eats out of the same pile, and the price is read straight off what is left: roughly two months' supply and the good sells at what it is worth, a week's supply and it is four times that, a glut and the price sinks to a quarter. No merchant sets it, no market maker smooths it.

    3. 03

      Distance decides the rest

      A merchant compares the price at his elbow with the price one road away, subtracts the haulage — which is charged by bulk, so spice travels and timber mostly does not — subtracts the toll, and goes if anything is left. Buying lifts the price he is paying. Selling drops the price he is getting. Enough merchants and the gap closes to roughly the cost of the journey, which is as near as the world gets to a single price for anything.

    The same date integer that draws the flow field on the front page, weaves the cloth in the Loom, cuts the gears in the Engine and founds the countries in the Chronicle lays out this map, names the towns, deals the goods and gives every merchant his purse. After that nobody is steering. There is no target price anywhere in the code, no equilibrium being solved for and no invisible hand written down — a few dozen small, entirely selfish decisions a day are the only mechanism, and the convergence on the chart is a result rather than an instruction. Push the tolls up to forty per cent and it stops happening, because at that point the journey costs more than the difference is worth, and the towns go back to being strangers.
