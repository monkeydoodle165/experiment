The Lamplighter

The Tollgate kept still and let the road come to it. This room goes out into the streets at the one time of day that will not wait. The sun is going down on a Victorian town, and every gas lamp in the district has to be burning before it is dark. You have a pole with a hook and a small flame on the end, a contract that counts the minutes, and an inspector who walks the streets a quarter of an hour after you should have finished. Plan the round, set out at sunset, and watch the town go dark around the lamps as you light them.

Day
—

Every day is a different town, year, district and wind. The calendar date sets the sunset and the moon.

What is real here and what is not

Gas street lighting, lamplighters with poles, lighting-up times reckoned from sunset, moon tables and inspectors are real. So is the sky: sunset, twilight and the moon are worked out for the town's latitude on that date. The districts, the lamps, the contracts and the fines are the day's number.

  1. 01

    Gas comes to the streets

    Gas lamps were first shown off on Pall Mall in 1807, and within twenty years most large towns in Britain had a gas company laying mains under the streets. A lamp was a glass lantern on an iron post with a small burner inside, fed through a cock that someone had to turn on every evening and off every morning.

  2. 02

    The round

    That someone was the lamplighter. Oil lamps had needed a ladder; gas lamps could be reached with a long pole carrying a hook to work the cock and a little flame to light the gas. A lamplighter had a fixed round of lamps, often well over fifty, went round them at a trot at dusk, came back at dawn to put them out, and in between cleaned the glass and replaced cracked panes.

  3. 03

    Lighting-up time and the moon

    Contracts set the hour by sunset, which moves by hours through the year and further the farther north you are. Many parishes saved money with a moon table, leaving some lamps unlit on the nights around full moon when the moon was up to do the work. Companies kept inspectors to walk the streets and report lamps that were dark.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    One small district laid out as a rough grid, a round of a few dozen lamps, one walking pace, and lighting that takes the same time at every post. The clock is local mean time and the sunset is good to a minute or two. The contract minutes, wages and fines are sensible guesses, not the terms of any real company.

Sixty-seven rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it lays out the streets, stands the lamps along them and cracks a pane or two; the calendar does the rest, and brings the dark down on its own time.
