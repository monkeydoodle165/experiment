The Sheepwash

The Lambing Fold kept the flock through the spring nights. This room is the end of that year's work on it, in June, when the sheep were washed in the river and then shorn. A pool penned off with hurdles or walled in stone, men up to their waists in it turning each sheep over and over until the water ran clear, and a week or two later the shearing gang, who went round the farms of a district on a set round. Wash too late and the gang is idle; clip too soon and the fleece is still wet and harsh and light; wait too long and the wool is dirty again, or the fly has found the flock. The river does not care about any of it, and a night's thunder can put it over the pool for days.

The hands wash as many as they can in a day, and the gang always takes the longest-washed sheep first. A fleece needs three days out of the pool before it is dry enough to clip at all, and is at its best from about a week to a fortnight, when the yolk has risen back into the wool; after three weeks it is as dirty as if it had never been washed. The gang only works the days it is booked to you, never on a wet day or the day after a soaking, and you cannot wash when the river is over the pool. From the second week, warm days bring the fly to sheep still in their wool. Whatever is not clipped by the last day is clipped in July, late and cheap. Keys: W wash, S shear, B both, 0 leave them. Point at the strip to see any day.

Day
—

Every day is a different river, flock and June: dry hot ones when the fly is the enemy, broken ones when the river is up and down all month, and wet cold ones when the gang stands about in the barn waiting for the sheep to dry.

What is real here and what is not

Washing sheep in the river before shearing, wash-pools and wash-folds, travelling shearing gangs and the fly are all real, and so are the rivers and the breeds: each is a place where flocks like these were washed in the nineteenth century. The farms, the gangs, the prices and the weather are the day's number.

  1. 01

    The wash

    Until well into the twentieth century most English and Welsh flocks were washed before they were shorn, because washed wool fetched a better price than wool sold in the grease. Villages had a sheepwash on the river, a pool dammed with boards or penned with hurdles, or a walled fold beside a beck, and the washing was a day's hard, cold, wet work for the men in the water, paid for partly in beer.

  2. 02

    The rising

    A washed fleece was not shorn straight away. The sheep were left a week or two for the wool to dry and for the natural grease, the yolk, to rise back into it, which made the fleece heavier, softer and easier to clip. Shear too soon and the wool was harsh and light; leave it too long and the sheep had rolled and rubbed it dirty again.

  3. 03

    The gang and the fly

    Shearing was often done by gangs of men who went from farm to farm round a district, paid by the score, so a farmer had their days and not others. Wet sheep could not be shorn, since damp wool heats and spoils in the bale. And as June warmed, blowflies laid their eggs in dirty, damp fleeces, and a struck sheep that was not found and treated could be eaten alive; getting the wool off was the best protection.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    Each day has one weather, the river answers to the rain with one rule, and the hands always wash a full batch. One number stands for a fleece's condition, set by the days since it was washed and by how thick the water was; the fly takes a small share of what is still in its wool on each warm day; and every price, pay and rate is a sensible guess, not the accounts of any real farm.

Seventy-three rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it chooses a river and a June, books the gang for the wrong week, and asks whether to wash in thick water or wait for a river that may not go down.
