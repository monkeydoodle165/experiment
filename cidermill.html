The Cider Mill

The Hop Garden got the hops in in September. This room is the harvest after it, October and November in the cider country of Hereford, Gloucester, Somerset and Devon, when the orchards drop their fruit into the grass and the women of the village go out with baskets to pick it up. The apples are tumped in heaps in the yard to sweat and mellow before the horse goes round the stone mill and the pomace is built into a cheese of straw under the press. Grind them straight off the grass and the cider is thin and sour. Leave them lying in the orchard and they rot, or a white frost has them in a night. Leave them in the tump too long and they go bad there instead, and there is only one horse, one press and one set of hands.

One crew, so one job a day. The women gather in one orchard, picking up what is lying first and then shaking and poling down what is ripe enough to come; they are slower in the rain and will not go out in heavy rain at all. The mill and press take the oldest tumps first and work under cover in any weather. A tump wants a week to ten days before it is worth grinding and is at its best to about eighteen days; after three weeks it starts to rot. Apples rot in the grass, faster when it is warm and wet; a frost spoils what is lying out and a hard one nips the tumps too; and a gale in the night brings down whatever is ripe. A glass that is high and clear means a cold night. Whatever is on the trees, in the grass or in the yard at Martinmas is lost. Keys: 1–5 gather in that orchard, P grind and press, 0 nobody works. Point at the strip to see any day.

Day
—

Every day is a different parish and autumn: dry still ones with clear cold nights when the frost is the enemy, soft wet ones when the grass rots everything lying in it, and rough ones when a gale can put a whole orchard on the ground before the yard has room for it.

What is real here and what is not

Cider orchards of standard trees, women gathering in the grass, tumps sweating in the yard, horse-drawn stone mills, screw presses with cheeses of straw, and the apple names are all real, and so are the parishes: each is a place where cider was made on the farm like this in the nineteenth century. The farms, the orchards, the crews, the prices and the weather are the day's number.

  1. 01

    Gathering

    Cider fruit was mostly left to fall and picked up off the grass, by women and children paid by the day or the bag, with the last of it shaken from the trees or knocked down with long poles. In wet autumns the grass rotted the fruit fast, and pigs, sheep, wasps and birds had their share; a hard frost turned what was lying out soft and brown.

  2. 02

    The tump

    Gathered apples were heaped in the yard or a loft to sweat for a fortnight or three weeks, until they were mellow enough to take a thumb. Sweating softened the fruit, ripened the starch into sugar and made a stronger, sweeter cider that kept; apples ground hard gave thin juice that soured. Left too long, the heaps rotted from the middle.

  3. 03

    The mill and the press

    A horse walked round a circular stone trough, turning an upright runner stone that crushed the apples to pomace. The pomace was built up in layers between straw or hair cloths into a cheese, screwed down under the press, and the juice ran off to ferment in hogsheads in the cellar. Much of the farm's cider went to the labourers as part of their wages.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    One crew does one job a day; each orchard drops its fruit on one smooth curve that the wind and frost push along; every tump sweats at the same pace and one number stands for the cider, set by how long the fruit sweated and the apple, and nothing else. There is no blending, no perry, no keeping fruit back for the market, and every rate, wage and price is a sensible guess, not the books of any real farm.

Seventy-five rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it drops two orchards in the same week, fills the yard with green fruit, and clears the sky on the night everything is lying in the grass.
