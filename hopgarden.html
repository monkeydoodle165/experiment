The Hop Garden

The Sheepwash took the wool off in June. This room is September, the other harvest of the southern counties, when whole streets of London and the Black Country went down by train to the hop gardens of Kent, Sussex, Surrey and Hereford, and families stripped the bines into bins at so many bushels to the shilling. The hops in a farm's gardens do not ripen together. Pick a garden green and the sample is light and pale; leave it a week past its best and it goes brown and blowsy and shatters in the bin. A wet warm spell lets the mould in, a gale can lay a whole garden on the ground in a night, and the oast can only dry so much a day.

The pickers strip one garden a day, as much as they can pick and the oast can dry, and are paid by the bushel. A garden is at its best from a day before it is ripe to about four days after; picked a week early it dries light and pale, and a week late it is brown and shatters. In rain they pick slower and the wet hops dry worse; in heavy rain they will not pick at all. Warm wet days let the mould into any garden near ripe, and the glass falling hard means wind in the night, which knocks down ripe bines. Stand them down on a fine day and some families go off to the next farm. Whatever is still on the bines when the pickers go home at the end is lost. Keys: 1–7 pick that garden, 0 stand them down. Point at the strip to see any day.

Day
—

Every day is a different parish and September: fine settled ones when the only enemy is the hops ripening faster than the pickers can strip them, close muggy ones when the mould races through the gardens, and broken windy ones when the glass is worth watching.

What is real here and what is not

Hop gardens, bins and tallymen, oast houses, the hopping trains from London and the hop varieties are all real, and so are the parishes: each is a place where hops were grown and picked like this in the second half of the nineteenth century. The farms, the gardens, the pickers, the prices and the weather are the day's number.

  1. 01

    The picking

    Hops grow up strings or poles on bines that are cut down at picking time and stripped by hand into canvas bins. The pickers were paid by the bushel, measured out of the bin by the tallyman, at a rate the farmer set each year as so many bushels to the shilling. In Kent most of them came down from London for the season, whole families living in huts or tents, and in Herefordshire they came from the Black Country and Wales.

  2. 02

    Ripe, and over

    A hop is ripe when the cone is firm, papery and rustles, and the yellow lupulin dusts the fingers. Picked green it dries light and lacks the resin the brewer pays for; left too long it browns, the cones open and shatter, and the sample is marked down. A farmer with several varieties and several gardens had to choose each morning which to strip, knowing he could not have them all at their best.

  3. 03

    The oast

    Green hops will heat and spoil in a day, so everything picked had to go up to the oast that night and be dried on the hair-cloth over a charcoal or anthracite fire, then cooled and pressed into pockets. The number of kilns set a hard limit on how much could be picked, whatever the pickers could manage.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    Each day has one weather, the pickers strip one garden at a time, and every garden ripens on one day. One number stands for the sample, set by how far from ripe it was picked, the wet, the mould and nothing else; the gale takes a share of what is near ripe; the price of hops, which swung wildly from year to year, is fixed for the season; and every rate and price is a sensible guess, not the books of any real farm.

Seventy-four rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it plants the gardens, ripens three of them in the same week, and drops the glass on the night you left the best of them standing.
