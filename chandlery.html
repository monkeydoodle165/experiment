The Chandlery

In the Saltpan the heat decided the grain. In a tallow chandler's back room it decides the candle. A wheel of rods hung with bare cotton wicks turns over a vat of melted fat, and every round of it dips every wick once. Each dip leaves a coat; forty or fifty coats make a candle. Keep the tallow just running, let each coat set before the next goes on, and know when to stop, because the order wants candles so many to the pound and the carrier will not wait.

Day
—

Every day is a different town, season, tallow, order and carrier.

What is real here and what is not

Dipping, the wheel, the warmth of the vat and of the room, the sizes so many to the pound and the candle duty are real. The town, the tallow, the order and the carrier are the day's number, and the candle itself is a small model.

  1. 01

    Dips and moulds

    Most candles burned in British houses before the 1850s were tallow dips: rendered beef and mutton fat built up coat by coat on a cotton wick. Wax was for churches and the rich, moulded tallow cost a little more, and dips were what the shop, the kitchen and the cottage used. Sizes were sold by count to the pound: twelves, eights, long sixes and fours. A chandler's wheel or frame carried many rods of wicks, so while one rod was down in the vat the rest were up in the air, cooling.

  2. 02

    Just hot enough to run

    Tallow kept only a little above its setting point leaves a thick coat at each dip, but it drags, ridges and builds layers that part. Hot tallow leaves a thin, smooth coat and, hotter still, melts off what was put on before; overheated tallow smokes and darkens. And each coat had to set before the next: in hot weather it would not, which is why chandlers dipped in the cold months and shut up shop, or worked at night, in summer. Mutton tallow sets harder and higher than beef, and makes the better candle.

  3. 03

    The exciseman

    From 1709 until 1831 candles were taxed, a penny a pound on tallow at the end of the period and much more on wax. A chandler had to give the excise notice before every making, and an officer could come in and weigh what was on the rods. Its repeal in 1831 was celebrated as a tax off the poor man's light.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    The coat each dip leaves is one smooth curve of how far the vat sits above the melting point, and the candle cools by one exponential whose pace grows with its thickness. Every candle on the wheel shares one history, and the spread of their weights is a rule of thumb from the ridges and sagging feet. Melting points, prices, wages and the room's warmth are sensible guesses for their period, not records.

Sixty-three rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it picks a town and a tallow, writes an order on the slate, and warms the room through the afternoon, so the pace that suited the morning will not suit the day.
