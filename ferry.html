The Ferry

The Maltings took ten days and never moved. This room is a morning on the water. A ferry crosses a tidal river from one stair to the other, and the river never stops sliding past under you. Point the bow straight at the stair and you will land a long way below it. Point her up into the stream, crab across, work up the slack water under the bank when you have been set down, and keep out of the way of whatever is coming down on the tide.

Every day is a different river, boat, tide, wind and run of passengers.

What is real here and what is not

The ferries, the stairs, crabbing across a stream, the slack water under the bank and wind against tide are real. The width, the stream, the passengers and the traffic are the day's number, and the river is a small model.

  1. 01

    Crabbing across

    A boat moves through the water, and the water moves over the ground. What you see from the bank is the sum of the two. To go straight across a stream you point the bow up into it, far enough that the part of your speed that goes upstream cancels the stream, and the boat crabs across sideways. Watermen and ferrymen did this all day without drawing a triangle. Aircraft pilots and swimmers in rip currents do the same sum.

  2. 02

    The slack under the bank

    A river runs fastest over its deep channel and slowest along the sides, where the bottom and the banks drag on it. A boat that has been set down below its stair does not fight back up the middle. It creeps up in the slack close inshore, then strikes out across. The tide also eases off to nothing at high and low water, and a crossing at slack water is an easy one.

  3. 03

    Ferries and stairs

    Most river ferries were held by ancient right, often by a manor or a borough, and leased to whoever rowed them. Passengers went down a public stair or slipway to the water, paid a penny or a halfpenny, and sat in the stern. Sailing barges, colliers and trows worked up and down on the tide, and after the 1830s steam tugs went wherever they liked. When wind blows against the tide the water stands up into a short, steep chop, and a laden boat goes slower and wetter.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    The river is straight, the same width all the way along, and its stream is one curve across the channel that rises and falls with a single tide. The boat answers its oars after a few seconds and tires your arms by one rule. Widths, fares, rents and traffic are sensible guesses for the period, not records of any real ferry.

Sixty-five rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it chooses the river, sets the time of high water and the strength of the stream, turns the wind against the tide or with it, and sends the passengers and the barges down to the water.
