The Tollgate

The Ferry was a morning on moving water. This room stands still and lets the road come to you. You keep a turnpike gate, and everything on the road has a price painted on the board: so much a horse for a coach, more for a waggon on narrow wheels, so much a score for cattle, extra for every hundredweight over on the weighing engine. Some of it goes through free, and some of what claims to go through free is lying. Count the horses, read the tickets, take the right money, and open the gate before the Mail has to sound its horn twice.

What is real here and what is not

Turnpike trusts, toll boards, broad and narrow wheels, weighing engines, tickets that clear the next gate and the usual exemptions are real. The roads are real roads; the gates, the rates and the travellers are the day's number.

  1. 01

    Turnpike trusts

    From the early 1700s Parliament let local trusts take over a stretch of road, borrow money to mend it, and charge everyone who used it. By the 1830s more than a thousand trusts kept some twenty thousand miles of road and nearly eight thousand gates. Each gate's tolls were usually let by auction for a year, and the lessee, or the keeper he put in the toll house, made his living from whatever he took over the rent.

  2. 02

    Wheels and weight

    Narrow wheels on a heavy waggon cut a road to pieces, so the law taxed them. Waggons on wheels six inches or more across paid less a horse and were allowed to carry more. Many gates had a weighing engine, a platform on a great steelyard, and charged by the hundredweight for anything over the limit.

  3. 03

    Who went free

    The General Turnpike Acts let a long list through without paying: the Royal Mail, soldiers on the march and their baggage, people going to church on a Sunday, funerals, dung for the land, stone to mend the road, and on many roads lime and horses going to plough. Keepers spent their days deciding whether a cart of "manure" really was, and the Post Office fined any who kept the Mail waiting.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    One board, one gate, one ticket arrangement, and travellers who tell the truth about everything you can see. Real boards ran to dozens of lines, charged differently for summer and winter, and argued about whether a gig was a chaise. The rates and limits are sensible guesses for the period, not the board of any real gate.

Sixty-six rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it paints the board, sets the year and the day of the week, and sends everyone down the road to your gate, the honest and the other kind.
