The Water Meadow

The Icehouse spent a winter hoarding cold. This room spends one keeping it off. In the chalk valleys of Wessex the farms flooded their meadows on purpose from December to March: a thin sheet of spring water, warmer than the air, kept running over the grass so that it went on growing while everything else stood still. You are the drowner. Each day you draw or drop the hatch on each of eight beds. Too little water and the frost browns the grass; too long under water and a bed goes sour and rots. In March the ewes and lambs come down off the downs, and the meadow has to feed them.

Every day is a different river, year and winter: some hard, with the meadow kept under water through weeks of frost, and some so mild that the beds sour in a few days.

What is real here and what is not

Floated water meadows, their hatches, carriers and drains, the drowner, the winter watering and the early grass for ewes and lambs are all real. The rivers and villages are real places where meadows were worked. The winters, the beds, the flocks and the prices are the day's number.

  1. 01

    Grass in March

    In the chalk country the hardest time for a sheep farm was early spring, when the hay was nearly gone and nothing grew on the downs. From the seventeenth century farmers in the valleys of Wiltshire, Hampshire, Dorset and Berkshire dug their riverside meadows into ridges and furrows and flooded them through the winter. The chalk springs run at much the same temperature all year, warmer than a winter night, so grass under the water kept growing.

  2. 02

    Carriers, hatches and drains

    A hatch on the river turned water into a main carrier, and smaller carriers ran along the tops of the ridges, or beds. Water spilled over their edges, ran down the slopes in a thin sheet and was gathered by drains in the furrows and led back to the river downstream. The drowner, a skilled man often paid by several farms together, opened and shut the hatches to water one part of the meadow after another.

  3. 03

    Moving, never standing

    Everything depended on the water moving. Left too long on a bed, especially in mild weather, it went stagnant, a scum formed and the grass rotted, so beds were watered for a week or two and then drained to rest. In hard frost the water was kept running, because a moving sheet does not freeze and protects the grass. Water from a river in flood carried silt that fed the meadow.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    The meadow is eight beds on one carrier, and the river covers some number of acres a day. Grass grows at a fixed rate under water, faster in thick water, and a little in the open on mild days. Sourness builds under water, faster when it is warm or deep, and drains away when the bed rests; frost browns dry beds and thin water. In real meadows the grass was grazed off by the sheep in spring and then shut up for a hay crop in June. The rates and prices are sensible guesses, not the records of any real farm.

Seventy rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it chooses a river and a winter, sends rain up on the downs and counts the ewes; what you keep growing in January is what the flock eats in March.
