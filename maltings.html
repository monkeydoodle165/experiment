The Maltings

The Chandlery was an afternoon against the clock. This room takes a week and a half. Barley comes out of the steep swollen and wet and is laid on a stone floor to start growing, and the growing makes heat. Thin the piece as it warms, turn it before the rootlets knit it into a mat, sprinkle it when it dries, and stop it on the kiln when the shoot inside each corn has crept three-quarters of the way up its back. Then the fire decides the colour.

The kiln

Pale malt is dried slowly and never let get hot; amber is finished warm; brown is blown over a fierce wood fire. Malt that goes on wet colours faster on a hot fire.

What is real here and what is not

Floor malting, the couch, turning, sprinkling, the acrospire rule, the kiln colours and the malt tax are real. The town, the barley, the weather and the order are the day's number, and the growing corn is a small model.

  1. 01

    Growing on the floor

    Barley was steeped in a cistern for two or three days, then laid on a malting floor to germinate. The rootlets break out of the bottom of the corn first; the shoot, the acrospire, grows up the back of the corn under the husk, and as it goes the starch ahead of it turns mealy and sweet. Maltsters split corns with a thumbnail to see how far it had got. Stop too early and the tip of the corn stays hard; let it grow out of the end and the corn has eaten what the brewer was paying for.

  2. 02

    Keeping it cool

    Growing grain breathes and warms, and a deep piece holds its heat. Maltsters thinned the floor as it warmed, thickened it when it was cold, worked the window shutters, and turned it with wooden shovels and ploughs several times a day so that the rootlets never knitted it into a felt. Slow, cool growth at the bottom of the fifties made the best malt. That is why floor malting was a winter trade and most maltings stood empty from June to September.

  3. 03

    The couch and the tax

    Malt was taxed from 1697 until 1880, and for most of the nineteenth century at about two and sevenpence a bushel. The officer charged it on his gauge of the grain in the cistern, the couch or the floor, whichever was greatest, and the law kept the barley in the couch frame for at least a day so it could be measured. Gladstone abolished the tax in 1880 and moved it onto beer.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    The whole floor is one temperature with one exponential for how fast it breathes, one curve for the spire and one for the rootlets, and the unevenness between corns is one number that turning shrinks. The kiln is a single finishing heat. Extract figures, prices and wages are sensible guesses for the period, not records.

Sixty-four rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it steeps a barley, sends a warm or a cold spell across the floor in the middle of the week, and names the day the kiln is wanted, so the growth you can afford to let run slow is never quite the growth the weather gives you.
