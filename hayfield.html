The Hayfield

The Ropewalk was one long walk under a roof, and the weather never came into it. Here the weather is the whole job. A meadow ready to cut, a gang of scythes, six working days and a weatherglass on the kitchen wall. Grass is three parts water when it falls; hay that goes into the rick too damp heats until it chars, and hay left spread out in the rain has its goodness washed out of it. All week long you are deciding what the sky will do next.

What is real here and what is not

The order of the work, the reason for each step and the way a falling glass runs ahead of the rain are real. The farm, the meadow and the week's weather are the day's number, and the drying is a small model.

  1. 01

    Making hay

    Before machines, a meadow was mown with scythes, a man taking about an acre a day, and the grass left in the swath where it fell. Women and boys followed with forks and tedded it, throwing it up and spreading it so the sun and wind could get through it, and turned it again later in the day. At evening, or when rain threatened, it was raked into windrows and put up in cocks, which shed most of the wet and dry hardly at all. When the hay rattled and a twisted wisp snapped instead of sweating, it was pitched onto the wagon and led to the rick.

  2. 02

    Why the rick heats

    Cut grass goes on living for a while, and so do the moulds and bacteria on it. Packed into a rick with too much water in it, their breathing warms the hay, and the warmth lets them breathe faster. A little sweat is good and gives hay its smell; too much turns it brown and mowburnt, and past about seventy degrees the heating can run on by itself until the middle chars or the rick catches fire. Farmers drove an iron bar into a new rick and felt it when it came out.

  3. 03

    The weatherglass

    An aneroid or mercury barometer reads the weight of the air. A depression coming in from the west drags the glass down ahead of its rain, bottoms out about when the worst of it passes, and lets it climb again behind. The words on the dial are much less use than the way the hand is moving, which is why a barometer has a second setting hand: you leave it where the glass stood and come back later to see which way it has gone. On this one the brass hand shows where the glass stood three hours ago. Summer thunder showers come with hardly any warning on the glass at all.

  4. 04

    What the page simplifies

    The gang works as one body on one land at a time, where a real farm would split it, and a job's state changes all at once when it finishes. The water in the hay moves toward what the air will allow at a rate set by the sun, the wind and how loosely it lies; rain adds to it in proportion to how open it lies and washes out some of its worth. The heating of the rick is worked out from how wet it went up, not from any real biology. The weather signs at dawn are right about three times in four, which is generous.

Sixty rooms, and one number doing all the work. Today it brings two or three depressions in off the sea, decides whether Thursday afternoon will thunder, and leaves you a glass to guess it from.
